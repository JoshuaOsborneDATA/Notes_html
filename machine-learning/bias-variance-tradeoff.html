Bias-Variance Tradeoff

The full MSE decomposition into bias squared, variance, and irreducible error.

by Joshua Osborne

recall from What is machine learning that no model we fit will ever produce a perfect prediction. The general form of our prediction is \(Y = f(X) + \epsilon\), where \(\epsilon\) is the error. It turns out there are two types of error: reducible and irreducible.

Reducible vs Irreducible Error

Irreducible error is the noise that exists no matter how good your model is. It comes from randomness or unmeasured variables that are fundamentally unknowable: things like measurement error, or factors that influence \(Y\) that we simply did not (or cannot) collect. No matter how well you fit \(f\), you can never get below this floor.

Reducible error is the part we can improve by building a better model. It has two components: bias and variance; understanding their tradeoff is one of the most important ideas in machine learning.

Notation

Symbol Type Meaning
\(Y\) scalar true value of the response variable
\(\hat{f}(X)\) or \(\hat y\) function our model’s prediction
\(\text{Bias}\) scalar how far the average prediction is from the truth
\(\text{Var}\) scalar how much predictions vary across different training sets
\(\epsilon\) scalar irreducible error (noise floor)
\(\sigma^2_\epsilon\) scalar variance of the irreducible error

Key Equations

below is the derivation for finding the total expected prediction error at a point \(x\) starting from the mean squared error. If you are unfamiliar with \(\mathbb{E}[X]\) please refer to the Expectation notes. Before we begin: a key part of this derivation relates to \(\epsilon\). That is, \(\epsilon\) represents the aggregate of many small, independent unmodelled influences, and by the central limit theorem that sum converges to a normal distribution such that \(\mathbb{E}[\epsilon]=0\)

Step 1: start with MSE and substitute \(Y = f(x) + \epsilon\)

\[\text{MSE} = \mathbb{E}\left[(Y - \hat{f}(x))^2\right] = \mathbb{E}\left[(f(x) + \epsilon - \hat{f}(x))^2\right]\]

Step 2: expand the square using \((a + b)^2 = a^2 + 2ab + b^2\), where \(a = f(x) - \hat{f}(x)\) and \(b = \epsilon\)

\[= \mathbb{E}\left[(f(x) - \hat{f}(x))^2\right] + 2\mathbb{E}\left[(f(x) - \hat{f}(x))\epsilon\right] + \mathbb{E}\left[\epsilon^2\right]\]

Step 3: the middle term vanishes because \(\epsilon\) is independent of \(\hat{f}(x)\) and has mean zero, so \(\mathbb{E}[\epsilon] = 0\) kills it. Also, since \(\mathbb{E}[\epsilon] = 0\), we have \(\mathbb{E}[\epsilon^2] = \text{Var}(\epsilon)\). This follows from the variance shortcut \(\text{Var}(X) = \mathbb{E}[X^2] - (\mathbb{E}[X])^2\): substituting \(\mathbb{E}[\epsilon] = 0\), we are left with \(\mathbb{E}[\epsilon^2] = \text{Var}(\epsilon)\). The irreducible error is now isolated:

\[= \mathbb{E}\left[(f(x) - \hat{f}(x))^2\right] + \text{Var}(\epsilon)\]

Step 4: add and subtract \(\mathbb{E}[\hat{f}(x)]\) inside the remaining term (the “add zero” trick):

\[f(x) - \hat{f}(x) = \underbrace{\left(f(x) - \mathbb{E}[\hat{f}(x)]\right)}_{A \ (\text{bias: a fixed constant})} + \underbrace{\left(\mathbb{E}[\hat{f}(x)] - \hat{f}(x)\right)}_{B \ (\text{deviation from mean: random})}\]

Step 5: expand \(\mathbb{E}[(A+B)^2]\):

\[\mathbb{E}\left[(A+B)^2\right] = \mathbb{E}[A^2] + 2\mathbb{E}[AB] + \mathbb{E}[B^2]\]

taking each term separately:

\(\mathbb{E}[A^2]\) — \(A\) is a constant so expectation does nothing: \[\mathbb{E}[A^2] = A^2 = \left(f(x) - \mathbb{E}[\hat{f}(x)]\right)^2\]

\(\mathbb{E}[AB]\) — \(A\) is a constant so pull it out, then \(\mathbb{E}[B] = 0\) kills it: \[\mathbb{E}[AB] = A \cdot \mathbb{E}[B] = A \cdot \mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}[\hat{f}(x)] - \hat{f}(x)\right] = A \cdot \left(\mathbb{E}[\hat{f}(x)] - \mathbb{E}[\hat{f}(x)]\right) = 0\]

\(\mathbb{E}[B^2]\) — this is exactly the definition of variance: \[\mathbb{E}[B^2] = \mathbb{E}\left[\left(\mathbb{E}[\hat{f}(x)] - \hat{f}(x)\right)^2\right] = \text{Var}(\hat{f}(x))\]

putting the three terms back together:

\[\mathbb{E}\left[(f(x) - \hat{f}(x))^2\right] = \underbrace{\left(f(x) - \mathbb{E}[\hat{f}(x)]\right)^2}_{\text{Bias}^2} + \underbrace{0}_{\text{cross term}} + \underbrace{\text{Var}(\hat{f}(x))}_{\text{Variance}}\]

Step 6: Put it all together:

\[\text{Total Error} = \underbrace{\left(f(x) - \mathbb{E}[\hat{f}(x)]\right)^2}_{\text{Bias}^2} + \underbrace{\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\hat{f}(x) - \mathbb{E}[\hat{f}(x)]\right)^2\right]}_{\text{Variance}} + \underbrace{\text{Var}(\epsilon)}_{\text{Irreducible}} \tag{1}\]

where the first two terms make up the reducible error and the last term is the irreducible error.

Bias

Bias is the error that comes from wrong assumptions in your model. A highly biased model is too simple to capture the true relationship in the data: it consistently misses in the same direction. This is called underfitting.

Think of it this way: if you tried to fit a straight line to data that follows a curve, your predictions would be systematically off regardless of how much data you had. That systematic gap is bias.

Variance

Variance is the error that comes from your model being too sensitive to the training data. A high-variance model learns the training data so well that it picks up on random noise, and when it sees new data it performs poorly. This is called overfitting.

A useful way to think about it: if you trained your model on several different samples of the data and got very different predictions each time, your model has high variance.

The Tradeoff

Bias and variance pull in opposite directions:

  • A simpler model (e.g. a straight line) tends to have high bias, low variance: consistently wrong, but stable

  • A more complex model (e.g. a high-degree polynomial) tends to have low bias, high variance: flexible enough to fit the truth, but sensitive to noise

The goal is to find the sweet spot that minimizes total reducible error. Crucially, no matter where you land on that tradeoff, you can never do better than the irreducible error \(\sigma^2_\epsilon\).
