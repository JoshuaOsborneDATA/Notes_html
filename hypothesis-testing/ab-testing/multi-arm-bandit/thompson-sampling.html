Thompson Sampling

Bayesian bandit: maintain a Beta posterior per arm, sample from it, pull the winner. Exploration emerges from uncertainty.

by Joshua Osborne

Thompson Sampling is a Bayesian approach to the multi-arm bandit problem. Rather than explicitly balancing exploration and exploitation (as in Epsilon greedy algorithm or Upper Confidence Bound), it does so naturally by maintaining a probability distribution over each arm’s true reward probability and sampling from it.

The Idea

For each arm, we maintain a belief about how good it is, modelled as a Beta distribution. At each round we:

  1. Sample one value from each arm’s Beta distribution
  2. Pull the arm with the highest sampled value
  3. Observe the reward (0 or 1)
  4. Update that arm’s Beta distribution with the new data

Over time, the distributions narrow around each arm’s true reward probability. The best arm will naturally get pulled more often as it converges to its true mean and standard deviation shrinks.

Why Beta Distributions?

The Beta distribution is defined on \([0, 1]\), making it a natural prior for a probability. It has two parameters:

  • \(\alpha\): counts successes (rewards)

  • \(\beta\): counts failures (no reward)

These are sometimes called pseudo-counts since \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) increase by exactly 1 with each observed success or failure.

To begin, we first have an uninformative prior, \(Beta(1,1)\), which becomes incrementally updated using the following rule:

\[\alpha \leftarrow \alpha + 1 \quad \text{(success)}\] \[\beta \leftarrow \beta + 1 \quad \text{(failure)}\]

This is the conjugate prior for the Bernoulli likelihood, meaning the posterior stays in the Beta family.

Worked Example

Suppose you are testing three email subject lines and want to maximize open rates. The true open rates are unknown to you:

Arm Subject line True open rate
A “Don’t miss out!” 30%
B “Your weekly update” 50%
C “We picked this for you” 70%

All arms start at \(\text{Beta}(1, 1)\); a flat prior, meaning we have no preference.

Round 1. sample one value from each arm’s distribution:

Arm Distribution Sample draw
A Beta(1,1) 0.42
B Beta(1,1) 0.61
C Beta(1,1) 0.55

→ Pull B (highest draw). The email is sent and the recipient opens it (reward = 1). Update: B becomes \(\text{Beta}(2, 1)\)

Round 2. B’s distribution has shifted slightly right:

Arm Distribution Sample draw
A Beta(1,1) 0.71
B Beta(2,1) 0.58
C Beta(1,1) 0.33

→ Pull A (highest draw). The email is sent and the recipient does not open it (reward = 0). Update: A becomes \(\text{Beta}(1, 2)\)

Round 3.

Arm Distribution Sample draw
A Beta(1,2) 0.28
B Beta(2,1) 0.74
C Beta(1,1) 0.69

→ Pull B (highest draw). The email is sent and the recipient opens it (reward = 1). Update: B becomes \(\text{Beta}(3, 1)\)

After many rounds, C will accumulate the most evidence of being the best arm and as such, Thompson Sampling will converge on pulling it most often without ever being explicitly told it was the best.

How This Displays Bayes’ Rule

Thompson Sampling is a direct, live application of Bayes Rule:

\[P(\theta \mid \text{data}) \propto P(\text{data} \mid \theta) \cdot P(\theta)\]

Bayes term Thompson Sampling meaning
Prior \(P(\theta)\) Beta(α, β); our current belief about arm’s reward probability
Likelihood \(P(\text{data} \mid \theta)\) Bernoulli; probability of observing the reward given θ
Posterior \(P(\theta \mid \text{data})\) Beta(α+1, β) or Beta(α, β+1); updated belief after the pull

The key insight is that Thompson Sampling never needs to explicitly decide when to explore vs exploit because the uncertainty in the Beta distributions does it automatically. A wide distribution (little data) is more likely to produce a high sample, which drives exploration. A narrow distribution (lots of data) produces samples close to the true mean, which drives exploitation.

Limitations

Stationarity assumption - Thompson Sampling assumes each arm’s true reward probability is fixed over time. If open rates change (e.g. seasonal effects, audience fatigue), the accumulated pseudo-counts become stale and the algorithm is slow to adapt.

Arms 1 and 2 may not converge - Thompson Sampling is designed to maximize cumulative reward, not to learn the true θ of every arm. Once the best arm establishes itself, the weaker arms receive very few pulls and their distributions stay wide and uncertain.

Binary rewards only (in this form) - the Beta-Bernoulli setup only applies when rewards are 0 or 1. Continuous rewards (e.g. revenue per click) require a different prior-likelihood pair.

Statistical Testing: IPW Correction

If you want to run a hypothesis test on arm means after a Thompson Sampling experiment, you cannot use the raw observed means.

Thompson Sampling pulls arms with unequal, time-varying probabilities. Arms that looked good early get pulled far more often, which creates a feedback loop. The naive mean \(\hat{\mu}_k = \frac{1}{n_k}\sum r_t\) is biased upward for arms that got lucky early and biased downward for arms that were initially underestimated. This is called adaptive data collection bias and it violates the i.i.d. assumption that hypothesis tests rely on.

Fix: Inverse Probability Weighting (IPW)

Re-weight each observation by the inverse of the probability that the arm was selected at that step. This cancels the over- or under-sampling introduced by the policy.

Symbol Type Meaning
\(T\) scalar total number of rounds
\(A_t\) random variable arm pulled at round \(t\)
\(R_t\) random variable reward observed at round \(t\)
\(\pi_t(k)\) scalar probability that Thompson Sampling selected arm \(k\) at round \(t\)

The IPW estimator for arm \(k\)’s true mean reward is:

\[\hat{\mu}_k^{\text{IPW}} = \frac{\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T} \frac{\mathbf{1}[A_t = k]}{\pi_t(k)}\, R_t}{\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T} \frac{\mathbf{1}[A_t = k]}{\pi_t(k)}}\]

The denominator normalizes the weights so the estimator is consistent even as \(\pi_t(k)\) changes across rounds. This is the Horvitz-Thompson form of the IPW estimator.

Why It Works

Weighting by \(1/\pi_t(k)\) gives observations from neglected arms more influence and down-weights observations from favored arms. In expectation this restores the unbiasedness that standard tests require:

\[\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{\mathbf{1}[A_t = k]}{\pi_t(k)} R_t\right] = \mu_k\]

Workflow

  1. During the experiment, log \(\pi_t(k)\) for every round. After sampling from each Beta distribution, record the probability the policy assigned to each arm. A practical way to do this is to run \(B\) samples from all arms’ Beta distributions and count the fraction that arm \(k\) wins.
  2. After the experiment, compute \(\hat{\mu}_k^{\text{IPW}}\) for each arm using the logged propensities.
  3. Plug the IPW-corrected means (and their variance estimates) into your hypothesis test in place of the raw means.

Propensity scores \(\pi_t(k)\) must be logged in real time. They cannot be reconstructed after the fact without the exact Beta parameters at each round.
